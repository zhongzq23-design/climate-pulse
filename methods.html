Methods & definitions

Climate Pulse separates three kinds of information that can look similar on a map: source-reported hazard metrics, Climate Pulse-derived estimates or context, and local climate background. The default rule is source first: when GDACS or an upstream hazard system publishes a usable metric, Climate Pulse reports that value. An equivalent metric is calculated only when the source value is unavailable, and the result is explicitly labelled as derived.

How to read this page: the sections below explain how events enter the map, how hazard-specific metrics are selected, how derived quantities are calculated, and what each quantity does—and does not—mean.

1. How Climate Pulse chooses a metric

Climate Pulse does not recalculate the same quantity by default. For each metric, the project follows a fixed authority order:

structured GDACS / upstream metric → Climate Pulse fallback estimate → optional supplemental context

This order preserves both provenance and meaning. A source-first value can still be modelled rather than directly observed, so the interface distinguishes exposure, impact and derived estimates explicitly.

Exposed populationPeople spatially inside a modelled hazard footprint or buffer. Exposure does not imply observed harm.
Affected populationUsed only when a source reports an actual impact. It is not inferred from a polygon overlay.
Displaced populationUsed only for source-reported displacement or evacuation-type impacts.
Estimated population exposedA Climate Pulse-derived spatial estimate used only when the upstream equivalent is unavailable.

2. How events enter the map

The Standard Events backend is scheduled three times per day, at 00:17, 08:17 and 16:17 UTC. Each run checks NASA EONET, GDACS and Copernicus CEMS Rapid Mapping, normalizes source records into a common event structure and archives the resulting snapshot before downstream enrichment.

  • NASA EONET — event discovery and source metadata.
  • GDACS — disaster records, alert levels, mapped hazard/event geometry and hazard-specific impact or exposure resources.
  • Copernicus CEMS — emergency mapping activations and related source metadata.
Publication on Climate Pulse means that a record passed the current operational rules. It does not mean Climate Pulse independently verifies every statement made by the upstream source.

3. Event identity and deduplication

Source IDs and links are retained so an event can be traced back to its origin. For non-wildfire hazards, close records from different feeds can be treated as duplicates when they have the same hazard type and occur within the current spatial and temporal thresholds:

same hazard type AND distance < 80 km AND |date₁ − date₂| ≤ 5 days

Wildfires use a stricter identity rule. Distinct GDACS wildfire event IDs remain separate even when they occur near one another at similar times. A wildfire record is deduplicated only when an explicit shared identity shows that two records refer to the same event, such as a CEMS record that directly references the same GDACS ID.

4. Mapped event footprints

When GDACS provides a usable polygon for a wildfire, tropical cyclone, drought or flood, Climate Pulse can show that geometry in the expanded event details. The browser map uses a topology-preserving simplified copy for readability, while Climate Pulse-derived spatial calculations use the unsimplified source geometry.

browser footprint = topology-preserving simplify(mapped source geometry)
derived spatial calculation = unsimplified mapped source geometry × reference dataset

The meaning of a polygon depends on the hazard. Wildfire perimeters and tropical-cyclone hazard zones can be used as exposure-grade geometry. GDACS flood polygons are treated as reported event/affected-area context, not observed inundation extent. Drought polygons are treated as risk/impact-area context, not confirmed crop loss.

If no usable source polygon exists, Climate Pulse does not invent one simply to fill the interface. Polygon-derived metrics are withheld when the required geometry is unavailable or fails quality control.

5. Wildfire: GDACS/GWIS metrics first

For each GDACS wildfire, Climate Pulse first reads the current GDACS episode and its GWIS impact resource. Burned area and population exposure are kept aligned to the same episode so a card does not mix metrics from different wildfire updates. The population fields are modelled exposure, not observed harm.

POPAFFECTEDPopulation exposed in the burned area; equivalent to SUMPOP0.0.
SUMPOP1.0Population within 1 km of the burned area.
SUMPOP2.0Population within 2 km.
SUMPOP5.0Population within 5 km.
SUMPOP10.0Population within 10 km.

If the corresponding GDACS/GWIS population field is unavailable, Climate Pulse can estimate exposure from the unsimplified mapped wildfire geometry and JRC GHSL 2025. The fallback is labelled as a Climate Pulse-derived estimate and never silently replaces a source value.

fallback exposed population = GHSL population inside unsimplified GDACS wildfire geometry

Wildfire identity and wildfire visibility are separate rules. Nearby wildfire IDs are not merged. For display, Orange and Red GDACS wildfires remain visible, while a Green wildfire must satisfy both current thresholds:

burned area ≥ 10,000 ha AND population within 5 km ≥ 10,000

MODIS forest overlap is retained only as supplemental spatial context and is not labelled as confirmed forest loss.

6. Tropical cyclones: advisory exposure first

For GDACS tropical cyclones, Climate Pulse reads the current episode's advisory timeline and prefers the current/actual advisory entry when available. The public exposure fields come from the structured advisory metrics rather than the ambiguous generic GDACS pop field.

pop39Population in the wind field of 39 kt or more.
pop74Population in the wind field of 74 kt or more.
popstormsurgePopulation in the modelled storm-surge zone.
actual / currentDescribes the advisory track entry; the associated population exposure remains modelled.

Only when structured advisory exposure is unavailable does Climate Pulse use a GDACS wind/impact polygon × GHSL fallback. EONET-only storms may use a clearly labelled 300-km proximity screen. Orange and Red GDACS storms remain visible.

A World Bank/GHSL GDP exposure proxy may also be shown as supplemental context. It is not a replacement for GDACS human-exposure metrics and is not an estimate of economic loss.

7. Drought: risk context, not crop-loss claims

GDACS drought classification draws on the Global Drought Observatory (GDO) and its agricultural drought risk/impact framework. When GDACS publishes an agricultural drought impact/risk area, Climate Pulse reports that source value first.

The public label is intentionally “Agricultural drought potential-impact/risk area”. It should not be interpreted as failed-crop area, crop-loss area or confirmed agricultural damage.

The current drought integration does not provide the same structured population-exposure fields that Climate Pulse uses for wildfire and tropical cyclone. Climate Pulse therefore does not manufacture an “affected population” from a drought polygon.

MODIS land/forest overlap and FAO CROPGRIDS crop physical area can be added as supplemental spatial context:

supplemental crop overlap = Σ CROPGRIDS crop physical area inside mapped drought risk/impact geometry

Green droughts keep the compact display focused on source severity/risk and mapped context. Orange and Red events may show additional asset context. A Red event can show a Human impact section, but values appear only when a reliable source provides them; any Climate Pulse-derived population estimate must be labelled Estimated population exposed.

8. Flood: source impacts and reported-area context

For floods, source-reported human impacts take precedence over any Climate Pulse spatial screening. When GDACS provides a usable flood polygon, Climate Pulse treats it as a reported event/affected-area context polygon, not as an observed inundation map.

That distinction also governs derived metrics. A population or GDP calculation made from the reported event area describes what lies inside that mapped context; it does not establish that every person, asset or location within the polygon was flooded.

Climate Pulse may calculate a country-split GDP exposure proxy from the mapped flood event area, GHSL 2025 population and World Bank WDI 2024 GDP per capita:

GDP exposure proxy = Σcountry [GHSL population in mapped flood event area × WDI 2024 GDP per capita]
This is supplemental context, not economic loss. It does not replace source-reported fatalities, displacement, affected population, asset damage or business interruption.

9. Reference datasets for fallback and context

Climate Pulse uses external reference datasets only when they serve a defined fallback or contextual role. They do not silently overwrite an equivalent upstream hazard metric.

Population fallbackJRC GHSL GHS-WUP-POP R2025A, epoch 2025, approximately 1 km.
Land / forest contextNASA MODIS MCD12C1.061, 2024, 0.05°, IGBP sub-pixel class percentages.
Crop contextFAO CROPGRIDS v1.08, 2020, 0.05°, crop physical area.
GDP contextWorld Bank WDI NY.GDP.PCAP.CD, 2024 GDP per capita in current US$.
The interface identifies whether a displayed value is source-reported/modelled, a Climate Pulse-derived fallback, or supplemental context.

10. Local climate background

The climate panel answers a different question from the event feed: what has the long-term climate background at this location looked like? It does not provide real-time weather and does not attribute an individual event to climate change.

The public view uses annual values from CRU-TS v4.10, a 0.5° global land dataset covering 1901–2025 except Antarctica. Climate Pulse extracts the nearest valid CRU land cell. If the reported event coordinate is offshore, the backend can use the nearest valid land cell within 500 km and reports that distance.

DroughtTemperature + precipitation + VPD
Wildfire / HeatTemperature + precipitation + VPD
Flood / Storm / LandslideTemperature + precipitation

Monthly anomalies and near-real-time weather are not used in the current public climate panel.

11. Long-term annual indicators

For each selected climate variable, Climate Pulse retains the 1901–2025 annual series and summarizes it with the same three quantities: an early baseline, a recent ten-year mean and a long-term linear trend.

Early baseline1901–1930 annual mean.
Recent annual mean2016–2025 annual mean.
Long-term trendOrdinary least-squares slope for 1901–2025, reported per century.
Change = mean(2016–2025) − mean(1901–1930)
Trend = OLS slope(annual value ~ year) × 100 years

The public chart shows annual values and a 5-year moving mean for readability. No significance badge or p-value is displayed.

12. CRU VPD calculation

Vapour pressure deficit (VPD) is a widely used measure of atmospheric dryness. It is the difference between saturation vapour pressure—the amount of water vapour air could hold at saturation at a given temperature—and actual vapour pressure. Higher VPD therefore indicates air that is farther from saturation and, in general, greater atmospheric evaporative demand.

CRU provides monthly actual vapour pressure (AVP) as vap. Climate Pulse calculates saturation vapour pressure (SVP) from monthly mean temperature T, then calculates monthly VPD before annual aggregation:

VPD = SVP − AVP
SVP = 6.1078 × exp(aT / (T + b))   [hPa]

For T ≥ 0°C, a = 17.269 and b = 237.3. For T < 0°C, a = 21.875 and b = 265.5.

VPD is closely related to relative humidity (RH). RH expresses actual vapour pressure as a percentage of saturation vapour pressure:

RH = 100 × AVP / SVP   [%]
VPD = SVP × (1 − RH / 100)

At a fixed temperature, lower RH corresponds to higher VPD. But SVP rises nonlinearly with temperature, so the same RH can correspond to a larger VPD in warmer air. RH and VPD are therefore related but not interchangeable measures of atmospheric moisture conditions.

Temporal-resolution limitation: calculating SVP from monthly mean temperature does not retain the diurnal temperature cycle or sub-daily asymmetric warming. The resulting VPD is used as long-term climate context, not as a high-frequency atmospheric-dryness metric.

See Zhong, Z., Chen, H.W., Dai, A. et al. (2025), “Sub-diurnal asymmetric warming has amplified atmospheric dryness since the 1980s,” Nature Communications 16, 8247 ↗.

13. Climate context is not event attribution

Climate Pulse uses the historical climate series to describe local background conditions and long-term change. A warming, drying or VPD trend at an event location does not, by itself, show that anthropogenic climate change caused that event.

Formal event attribution requires separate, event-specific evidence and is treated as a distinct information layer. Climate Pulse therefore keeps event detection, source impact/exposure metrics, Climate Pulse-derived fallback/context, local climate background and formal attribution conceptually separate.

14. Main limitations

  • GDACS event records, episodes, mapped polygons and impact resources can change as an event evolves.
  • GDACS/GWIS wildfire and tropical-cyclone population values are modelled exposure, not observed harm.
  • Different hazard polygons have different meanings; a flood reported-area polygon is not equivalent to an observed inundation map, and a drought risk area is not confirmed crop loss.
  • Browser footprints are simplified for display; Climate Pulse-derived spatial calculations use unsimplified source geometry.
  • The non-wildfire proximity rule is an operational cross-source deduplication heuristic; source IDs and links are retained for traceability.
  • Gridded residential population does not represent real-time human presence.
  • Forest and crop overlap describe spatial context, not ecological damage, crop failure or yield loss.
  • The GDP indicator is a country-level per-capita exposure proxy and does not represent observed economic loss or within-country economic concentration.
  • CRU-TS is land-only; offshore events may use the nearest valid land cell within the configured search distance.
  • CRU-derived VPD from monthly mean temperature omits sub-daily temperature variability.
