Methods & definitions

Climate Pulse publishes simple operational definitions so readers can see how events, exposure estimates and climate-context indicators are constructed. This page summarizes the logic in plain language and formulas rather than reproducing source code.

3× daily monitoring GHSL 2025 population CRU-TS v4.10 · 1901–2025 Theil–Sen + Mann–Kendall Context ≠ attribution

1. Event monitoring

The Standard Event backend checks NASA EONET, GDACS and Copernicus CEMS Rapid Mapping approximately three times per day, at 00:17, 08:17 and 16:17 UTC. Source records are normalized into a common event structure and archived before publication on the map.

  • NASA EONET — event discovery and source metadata.
  • GDACS — disaster records, alert levels and hazard geometry.
  • Copernicus CEMS — human-triggered emergency mapping activations.
A record appearing on Climate Pulse means that it passed the current operational rules. It does not mean that Climate Pulse independently verifies every impact statement in the source record.

2. Deduplication and map clustering

Source IDs and source links are retained. Obvious cross-source duplicates may be merged when they describe the same hazard type and occur close together in space and time.

candidate duplicate if: same hazard type AND distance < 80 km AND |date₁ − date₂| ≤ 5 days

Major wildfire records may also be grouped visually when they occur within roughly 180 km and 7 days. A number inside a wildfire marker therefore means “grouped reports”, not severity.

3. Wildfire selection and population exposure

The main-map gate for GDACS wildfire records is currently burned area ≥ 10,000 ha. The threshold reduces map clutter and does not imply that a smaller fire cannot be locally severe.

GDACS major wildfire: burned area ≥ 10,000 ha

Where a usable GDACS fire polygon exists, it is overlaid with JRC GHSL GHS-WUP-POP R2025A population at approximately 1 km resolution.

Direct exposurePopulation inside the mapped wildfire footprint.
Nearby exposure (≤5 km)Additional population outside the footprint but within 5 km.
Total potential exposurePopulation in the footprint plus its 5 km buffer.
Direct = P(F)
Nearby ≤5 km = P(F ⊕ 5 km) − P(F)
Total = P(F ⊕ 5 km)
Exposure is not observed harm. These values do not equal casualties, evacuations, smoke exposure, property loss or confirmed personal impacts.

4. Tropical cyclones and storms

A cyclone centre point is not treated as the impact area. GDACS tropical-cyclone wind/impact polygons are preferred when available and are overlaid with GHSL population.

  • GDACS Orange/Red: retained on the main map.
  • GDACS Green: retained when the mapped TC footprint intersects populated cells.
  • EONET-only storm: a 300 km population proximity screen is used only as a fallback when a standard wind footprint is unavailable.
  • CEMS storm activation: retained because it represents an emergency-mapping activation.
TC potential exposure = P(mapped tropical-cyclone wind / impact footprint)

The 300 km fallback is labelled screening; it is not presented as a wind-impact estimate.

5. Drought population exposure

For GDACS droughts, Climate Pulse uses the latest GDACS drought event polygon rather than a generic radius. GDACS drought polygons are produced from persistent drought-risk patches and are reviewed as event extents before publication. Climate Pulse overlays the mapped event footprint with the same GHSL 2025 population grid used for other hazards.

Drought potential population exposure = P(D)

Here, D is the mapped GDACS drought event polygon and P(D) is the summed residential population inside that footprint. No additional distance buffer is added because drought is already represented as an areal, long-duration hazard.

Potential exposure is not the same as people affected. The value does not measure water shortage severity, crop loss, food insecurity, vulnerability, humanitarian need or confirmed impacts.

6. Population reference

Population exposure uses the European Commission Joint Research Centre GHSL GHS-WUP-POP R2025A, epoch 2025. Event calculations use the authoritative approximately 1 km source in the backend. A lighter 0.1° derivative is kept in the repository for fast screening and visualization.

7. Event climate context

The current public view uses annual climate context only. Climate background is extracted from the nearest valid land cell in CRU-TS v4.10, a 0.5° global land dataset covering 1901–2025. If the reported coordinate falls over the ocean, the backend may use the nearest valid CRU land cell within 500 km and reports that distance on the website.

Variables shown by hazard type

DroughtTemperature + precipitation + VPD
Wildfire / HeatTemperature + precipitation + VPD
Flood / Storm / LandslideTemperature + precipitation

This selection is a contextual display rule. It does not imply that the displayed variables alone explain the event. Monthly anomalies and near-real-time weather are not used in the current public climate panel.

8. Long-term annual indicators

Each event keeps the full 1901–2025 annual series for its selected variables. The public summary includes an early baseline, a recent ten-year mean and a robust long-term trend.

Early baseline1901–1930 annual mean.
Recent annual mean2016–2025 annual mean.
Long-term trendTheil–Sen median slope for 1901–2025, reported per century.
Change since early baseline = mean(2016–2025) − mean(1901–1930)
βTS = median[(Xⱼ − Xᵢ) / (yearⱼ − yearᵢ)], for all i < j
Trend per century = βTS × 100

The Theil–Sen estimator is used because it is less sensitive to individual extreme years than ordinary least squares. For precipitation, the trend can also be expressed as a percentage of the 1901–1930 mean.

Trend significance

A two-sided Mann–Kendall test is applied to the annual series. The website labels a trend “significant” when p < 0.05 and also displays the p-value.

Statistical caveat: the current Mann–Kendall p-value is the classical test and is not corrected for serial autocorrelation. It should therefore be treated as a descriptive significance indicator rather than a formal detection-and-attribution result.

The public charts show faint annual values, a 5-year moving mean for readability, and the Theil–Sen trend as a dashed line. Temperature and precipitation use separate axes; VPD, when relevant, is shown in a separate chart rather than forcing three y-axes into one panel.

9. CRU VPD calculation

CRU provides monthly actual vapour pressure (AVP) as vap. Saturation vapour pressure (SVP) is calculated from CRU monthly mean temperature T, then VPD is calculated before monthly values are aggregated into annual values.

VPD = SVP − AVP
SVP = 6.1078 × exp(aT / (T + b))   [hPa]

For T ≥ 0°C, a = 17.269 and b = 237.3. For T < 0°C, a = 21.875 and b = 265.5.

Temporal-resolution limitation: SVP is nonlinear in temperature. Calculating VPD from monthly-mean temperature cannot reproduce the effects of the diurnal temperature cycle or sub-daily asymmetric warming. This CRU-derived VPD is therefore a long-term climate-context metric, not a high-frequency VPD estimate.

See Zhong, Z., Chen, H.W., Dai, A. et al. (2025), “Sub-diurnal asymmetric warming has amplified atmospheric dryness since the 1980s,” Nature Communications 16, 8247 ↗.

10. Climate context is not attribution

A warming, drying or VPD trend can provide useful local background information, but it does not by itself demonstrate that anthropogenic climate change caused a particular wildfire, flood, cyclone, drought, heatwave or landslide.

Climate Pulse keeps event detection, population exposure, local climate context and formal event attribution as separate concepts.

11. Main limitations

  • Hazard polygons and event coordinates can change as an event evolves.
  • Gridded residential population is not real-time human presence.
  • Drought footprint exposure does not measure drought severity or vulnerability.
  • Cross-source deduplication and wildfire clustering use operational distance/time heuristics.
  • Grouped-event member sums can double-count population where footprints overlap.
  • CRU is land-only; offshore events may use the nearest valid land cell and that distance must be considered.
  • Classical Mann–Kendall significance is not corrected for serial autocorrelation.
  • CRU-derived VPD from monthly means omits sub-daily temperature variability.
