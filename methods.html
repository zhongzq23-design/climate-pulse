Methods & definitions

Climate Pulse is designed to be transparent about how events are selected and how population exposure and local climate context are estimated. This page explains the operational rules in plain language and simple formulas. It is a methods summary, not raw source code.

3× daily monitoring Source-aware event records GHSL 2025 population CRU-TS v4.10 climate Exposure ≠ observed harm

1. Monitoring and source data

The backend checks the current Standard Event sources three times per day, at approximately 00:17, 08:17 and 16:17 UTC. New and updated records are normalized into a common event structure, archived, and then used by the public map.

  • NASA EONET — natural-event discovery and event metadata.
  • GDACS — disaster events, alert levels, wildfire and tropical-cyclone geometry.
  • Copernicus CEMS Rapid Mapping — human-triggered emergency mapping activations.

Source ingestion does not mean that Climate Pulse independently confirms every impact statement in the source record.

2. Event normalization and deduplication

Each source keeps its original source ID and source link. To reduce obvious cross-source duplication, the current prototype may merge two records when they describe the same hazard type, occur within about 80 km, and are no more than 5 days apart.

candidate duplicate if: same hazard type AND distance < 80 km AND |date₁ − date₂| ≤ 5 days

This is an operational heuristic, not a claim that all nearby events are physically identical. Source IDs and links are retained so the merged record remains auditable.

Map-level wildfire clustering

Nearby major wildfire records may also be grouped visually on the world map when they occur within roughly 180 km and 7 days. This reduces map clutter. The underlying events are not deleted.

A cluster number such as “3” means three event records are grouped for display. It is not a severity score.

3. Wildfire selection

For GDACS wildfire candidates, the main-map gate is currently burned area ≥ 10,000 hectares. Smaller GDACS fires remain outside the main map. Copernicus CEMS wildfire activations may still be retained because they are already human-triggered emergency mapping cases.

GDACS major wildfire: burned area ≥ 10,000 ha

The threshold is a display and monitoring rule. It does not imply that a smaller fire cannot be locally severe.

4. Wildfire population exposure

For a wildfire with a usable GDACS polygon, Climate Pulse overlays the mapped wildfire footprint with the European Commission JRC GHSL GHS-WUP-POP R2025A 2025 population grid at approximately 1 km resolution.

Direct exposurePopulation inside the mapped wildfire footprint.
Nearby exposure (≤5 km)Additional population outside the footprint but within 5 km of its boundary.
Total potential exposurePopulation inside the footprint plus the surrounding 5 km buffer.
P(A) = Σ pᵢ for GHSL population cells selected inside area A
Direct = P(F)
Nearby ≤5 km = P(F ⊕ 5 km) − P(F)
Total potential exposure = P(F ⊕ 5 km)

Here, F is the mapped wildfire footprint and ⊕ 5 km means the footprint is expanded outward by 5 km before population is summed. The buffer is calculated in a metric projected coordinate system.

Important: population exposure is not the same as casualties, evacuations, smoke exposure, property loss, or confirmed personal harm. It describes where people live relative to the mapped hazard footprint.

5. Tropical cyclones and storms

Storms are treated differently from wildfires because a storm centre point alone does not describe the impact area.

  • GDACS tropical cyclone: use a tropical-storm/wind polygon when available and overlay it with GHSL population.
  • GDACS Orange or Red: retain on the main map even when the current population estimate is zero.
  • GDACS Green: retain when the mapped cyclone footprint intersects populated cells.
  • EONET-only storm: when no standard wind footprint is available, use a 300 km population proximity screen around the reported centre.
  • CEMS storm activation: retain because it is already a human-triggered emergency mapping activation.
TC potential exposure = P(mapped tropical-cyclone wind / impact footprint)
EONET fallback screen = P(circle of radius 300 km around reported centre)

The 300 km fallback is explicitly labelled screening. It is not presented as a wind-impact estimate.

6. Alerts and Climate Pulse priority

A source alert and the Climate Pulse display priority are related but not identical concepts. For GDACS records, Green / Orange / Red are source alert levels. Climate Pulse currently maps these approximately to Standard / Medium / High display priority while retaining the original source alert.

Priority affects presentation. It should not be interpreted as a universal physical severity scale across different hazard types.

7. Population data

The default population reference is the European Commission Joint Research Centre GHSL GHS-WUP-POP R2025A, epoch 2025. Event exposure calculations use the authoritative approximately 1 km source raster in the backend. A lighter 0.1° derivative is stored in the repository for rapid screening and visualization.

GHSL population product ↗

Using one population epoch provides a consistent global reference, but it cannot represent temporary travel, evacuations, commuting, seasonal population change, or local population change after 2025.

8. Grouped events and double counting

When multiple wildfire records are grouped, Climate Pulse may show a member sum. A member sum adds the exposure values of the individual records.

member-sum exposure = exposure₁ + exposure₂ + … + exposureₙ

If event footprints or 5 km buffers overlap, the same population can be counted more than once. Therefore a member sum is a population-exposure count, not a unique-person count.

Future weekly, monthly and annual “unique population exposed” products should instead spatially union the relevant hazard footprints before population is summed.

9. CRU climate context and VPD

For long-term local climate context, Climate Pulse uses the current CRU-TS v4.10 release from the Climatic Research Unit, University of East Anglia. CRU-TS v4.10 covers 1901–2025 over global land areas except Antarctica on a 0.5° × 0.5° grid. The repository stores one compact annual NetCDF file per year containing temperature, precipitation, actual vapour pressure and derived VPD.

Temperature · tmpAnnual calendar-day-weighted mean of monthly mean temperature (°C).
Precipitation · preAnnual sum of monthly precipitation totals (mm/year).
Actual vapour pressure · vapAnnual calendar-day-weighted mean of monthly CRU vapour pressure (hPa).
VPDCalculated from each month first, then calendar-day-weighted to an annual mean (hPa).

Monthly VPD calculation

CRU provides actual vapour pressure (AVP) directly as the monthly vap variable in hPa. Saturation vapour pressure (SVP) is calculated from CRU monthly mean temperature T in °C, and monthly VPD is then calculated before annual aggregation.

VPD = SVP − AVP
SVP = 6.1078 × exp(aT / (T + b))   [hPa]

For T ≥ 0°C, a = 17.269 and b = 237.3. For T < 0°C, a = 21.875 and b = 265.5. AVP is the CRU monthly vap value. The derived VPD is not artificially clipped before annual aggregation.

Annual aggregation

Annual mean X = Σ(Xₘ × daysₘ) / Σ(daysₘ), for X = tmp, vap, VPD
Annual precipitation = Σ monthly precipitation
Temporal-resolution limitation: the Climate Pulse CRU VPD product calculates SVP from monthly-mean temperature. Because saturation vapour pressure is nonlinear in temperature, SVP calculated from a monthly mean is not equivalent to the mean of SVP calculated from sub-daily temperatures. The product therefore omits effects of the diurnal temperature cycle and sub-daily asymmetric warming. It should be interpreted as coarse long-term climate context rather than a high-frequency VPD estimate.

This limitation is especially relevant to the role of asymmetric daytime/nighttime warming discussed by Zhong et al. (2025), “Sub-diurnal asymmetric warming has amplified atmospheric dryness since the 1980s,” Nature Communications 16, 8247 ↗.

CRU source and background: CRU-TS v4.10 ↗ · Harris et al. (2020) ↗.

10. Climate context is separate from attribution

Climate Pulse can show local long-term temperature, precipitation, vapour-pressure and VPD context at event locations. A local long-term trend can provide context, but it does not by itself establish that climate change caused a particular wildfire, flood, storm or other event.

The site therefore keeps event detection, population exposure, local climate context and event attribution as separate concepts.

11. Main limitations

  • Hazard polygons can change as an event evolves and can contain mapping uncertainty.
  • Population is represented by a gridded residential estimate, not real-time human presence.
  • Cross-source deduplication and map clustering use operational distance/time heuristics.
  • Exposure does not measure vulnerability, damage, mortality, evacuation or economic loss.
  • Different hazards require different footprints and should not be forced into one universal radius.
  • CRU VPD is derived from monthly means and does not retain sub-daily or diurnal temperature variability.
